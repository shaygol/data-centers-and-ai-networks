How to play

What the game is. JR-16 is a small Jupiter-style Clos fabric: 4 spine switches on top, 4 aggregation switches in the middle and 8 ToR (top-of-rack) switches at the bottom, one cable per connected pair. The map shows the plan, the cables that should exist. Five faults are hidden in the real network: a cable can be down, plugged into the wrong port, work in one direction only, or have a high error rate, and a switch can stop answering.

Your job. Find all five faults, take the broken links out of routing without draining links that work, and explain two of the faults.

  1. Collect. Press the button. Every switch reports which of its ports are up, and you get the list of links that are down. Down tells you that a link is broken, not why, and some faults leave the link up.
  2. Inspect. Click a switch on the map, then click each of its ports. You see what Neighbor Discovery found: who answered at the other end, whether the peer hears this switch, the PHY state and the error rate. Give every port a diagnosis (keys 1–6). Use How to choose under the port if you are unsure.
  3. Drain. Opens once all 62 ports are diagnosed. Click a cable on the map to drain it (click again to put it back), then press Recompute routes to see how many paths are left and whether any ToR lost its uplinks.
  4. Explain. Answer two multiple-choice questions on the cause of two faults.

Rules. Spines are on top, ToRs at the bottom. A cable has two ends, and a fault can look different from each one, so check both. A switch that does not answer has no port table: diagnose it from its neighbors’ ports. Draining a working link costs capacity, so drain only what you found broken. The game gives no hints about what is right until the deadline. Your work is saved in this browser and sent with the one Submit at the bottom of the page. There is no grade.         Firepath on the SDN planes, about 15 minutes

        Software-defined networking (SDN) moves forwarding decisions out of the individual switches and into software that sees the whole network. It uses three terms, the planes:

        • Data plane. Forwards each packet by looking it up in a table in the switch hardware.
        • Control plane. Decides what goes into those tables, for example by computing shortest paths.
        • Management plane. Configures, monitors and upgrades the devices.

        Traditional networks run the control plane on every switch: each one discovers its neighbors, exchanges routing messages (OSPF, IS-IS, BGP) and computes its own tables. SDN, as OpenFlow defined it, moves the control plane to a logically centralized controller, which computes the forwarding entries and installs them in each switch’s flow table. (N. McKeown et al., “OpenFlow: Enabling Innovation in Campus Networks,” SIGCOMM CCR 38(2), 2008; reference 18 in Jupiter.)

        Google built its own control plane, Firepath, long before “SDN” was a common term. In the game you saw its master collect link state and send it back out. Now place its parts on the planes.
